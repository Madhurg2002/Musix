# 05 Rhythm and Meter

**Rhythm** is the organization of time in music — when events happen, how long they last, and how they are grouped. **Meter** is the recurring pattern of strong and weak beats that gives a piece its rhythmic framework.

This module is instrument-agnostic and independent of pitch content.

## 1. Time, Duration, and Pulse

### Pulse

The **pulse** (or beat) is the underlying, recurring temporal unit — the "heartbeat" against which other durations are measured. A pulse may be explicit (as in a metronomic beat) or implied.

### Tempo

**Tempo** is the speed of the pulse, usually expressed in beats per minute (BPM). A tempo marking of 120 BPM means 120 pulses per minute, or 2 pulses per second.

Tempo marks (e.g., *Adagio*, *Allegro*, *Presto*) provide expressive indications, often with approximate BPM ranges, but the actual pulse rate may vary within a piece.

### Duration

A **duration** is the length of a musical event in time. Durations are related to the pulse through proportional division:

- A longer duration spans multiple pulses.
- A shorter duration is a subdivision of a pulse.

## 2. Note Values

In standard notation, relative durations are represented by note shapes. These shapes define durations relative to one another, not absolute time:

| Symbol (concept) | Name           | Relative duration              |
|------------------|----------------|--------------------------------|
| 𝅝                | Whole note     | 4 beats (in 4/4)              |
| 𝅗𝅥               | Half note      | 2 beats (in 4/4)              |
| 𝅘𝅥                | Quarter note   | 1 beat (in 4/4)               |
| 𝅘𝅥𝅮               | Eighth note    | Half a quarter note           |
| 𝅘𝅥𝅯               | Sixteenth note | Quarter of a quarter note     |

Dots and ties extend durations:

- A **dotted** note adds half of its base value (dotted quarter = quarter + eighth).
- A **tie** connects two notes of the same pitch into a single sustained duration.

These relationships are purely proportional. The same shapes can be used at any tempo.

## 3. Rests

**Rests** are the silent counterparts of note values. For every note value, there is a corresponding rest:

| Note value       | Rest            |
|------------------|-----------------|
| Whole note       | Whole rest      |
| Half note        | Half rest       |
| Quarter note     | Quarter rest    |
| Eighth note      | Eighth rest     |
| Sixteenth note   | Sixteenth rest  |

Rests are as important rhythmically as sounds — they create space, phrasing, and syncopation.

## 4. Time Signatures

A **time signature** indicates how beats are organized in each measure:

- **Top number:** how many beats per measure.
- **Bottom number:** which note value represents one beat.

For example:

- **4/4:** four quarter-note beats per measure — the most common "common time."
- **3/4:** three quarter-note beats per measure — waltz meter.
- **6/8:** six eighth-note beats per measure — often felt in two groups of three.

The bottom number uses a code:

| Value | Note value        |
|-------|-------------------|
| 1     | Whole note        |
| 2     | Half note         |
| 4     | Quarter note      |
| 8     | Eighth note       |
| 16    | Sixteenth note    |

## 5. Simple, Compound, and Complex Meters

### Simple Meter

In **simple meter**, each beat divides into two:

- 2/4, 3/4, 4/4 are simple meters.
- A beat feels like a single pulse divisible into two equal parts.

### Compound Meter

In **compound meter**, each beat divides into three:

- 6/8, 9/8, 12/8 are common compound meters.
- The beat is typically a dotted note value (e.g., dotted quarter in 6/8).

6/8, for example, is often felt in **two** main beats, each split into three eighth notes.

### Complex / Additive Meter

**Complex meters** have irregular groupings that don't fit simple duple or triple patterns:

- Examples: 5/8, 7/8, 11/8.
- These may be felt as combinations such as 2+3 or 3+2+2.

Complex meters are common in contemporary classical, folk, progressive, and world music traditions.

## 6. Strong and Weak Beats

Meter organizes beats into patterns of relative stress:

- **Strong beats** feel accented, expected, and stable.
- **Weak beats** feel lighter and tend to resolve toward strong beats.

In 4/4, for example:

- Beat 1 is strongest.
- Beat 3 is secondary strong.
- Beats 2 and 4 are weaker.

In 3/4:

- Beat 1 is strong.
- Beats 2 and 3 are weaker.

These stress patterns shape phrasing, groove, and harmonic rhythm.

## 7. Downbeats and Upbeats

- The **downbeat** is the first beat of a measure — typically the strongest.
- An **upbeat** (or anacrusis) is a note or group before the first downbeat, leading into the measure.

Upbeats create forward motion and are central to syncopation.

## 8. Syncopation

**Syncopation** places emphasis on weak beats or off-beats — moments that would normally be unstressed. This creates rhythmic tension and groove.

Common forms of syncopation:

- Accenting an off-beat (the "and" between main pulses).
- Lengthening a note across a strong beat so the accent falls on the weak side.
- Starting or ending phrases on off-beats.

Syncopation is central to jazz, funk, Latin music, rock, and many other styles.

## 9. Subdivisions

**Subdivision** breaks the beat into smaller units, defining rhythmic precision and feel:

- Duple subdivision: beat splits into 2, 4, 8, 16, etc.
- Triple subdivision: beat splits into 3 (common in swing, compound meter, and certain groove styles).
- Polyrhythms and cross-rhythms occur when different parts subdivide or group beats differently at the same time.

Feeling and controlling subdivisions is fundamental for both composition and performance.

## 10. Tuplets

A **tuplet** fits a number of notes into the space normally occupied by a different number:

- **Triplet:** 3 notes in the time of 2.
- **Quintuplet:** 5 notes in the time of 4 (or another indicated ratio).

Tuplets create temporary subdivision shifts and are written with a bracketed number.

## 11. Polyrhythm

A **polyrhythm** is the simultaneous use of two or more conflicting rhythmic groupings. Classic examples:

- 3 against 2 (a triplet feel over duple pulse)
- 4 against 3

Polyrhythms are written and felt as an alignment of different subdivisions over the same underlying pulse or measure.

## 12. Phrasing and Rhythmic Motifs

Rhythm is not just note lengths; it's also **phrasing** — how durations and accents group into larger units.

A **rhythmic motif** is a short, recognizable pattern of durations and accents. Motifs can be:

- Repeated, sequenced, inverted, or augmented/diminished in time.
- Developed much like a melodic motif, even without pitch change.

Phrasing is shaped by:

- Where accents fall within the meter.
- Where breaths, pauses, and phrase endings occur.
- How rhythmic patterns contrast and return.

## 13. Swing and Feel

**Swing** is a rhythmic convention in which equal written subdivisions (often eighth notes) are performed unevenly — typically with the first of each pair lengthened and the second shortened.

Swing is a **feel** rather than a strict notation, and its degree can vary from subtle to extreme. It is related to, but not identical to, triplet-based subdivisions.

## 14. Meter and Metric Modulation

- **Metric modulation** changes the pulse by reinterpreting a subdivision or note value as the new beat. For example, a dotted quarter might become the new quarter pulse.
- This allows smooth tempo changes and cross-references between metric feels.

Metric concepts are widely used in contemporary composition and ensemble coordination.

## 15. Rhythm Without Pitch

Rhythm stands on its own. Percussion, spoken word, and purely temporal structures all rely on the same principles:

- Pulse, tempo, and duration.
- Meter and stress patterns.
- Subdivisions, tuplets, syncopation, and polyrhythm.

A complete rhythmic understanding supports composition, analysis, and performance independently of pitch material.

## 16. Summary

- Rhythm organizes time; meter organizes beats into recurring strong/weak patterns.
- Tempo sets pulse speed; note values and rests define proportional durations.
- Time signatures show beats per measure and the beat unit.
- Simple meters divide beats in two; compound meters divide beats in three; complex meters use irregular groupings.
- Syncopation, subdivisions, tuplets, and polyrhythms add richness beyond the basic grid.
- Phrasing, motifs, and feel connect rhythmic details into expressive musical time.
