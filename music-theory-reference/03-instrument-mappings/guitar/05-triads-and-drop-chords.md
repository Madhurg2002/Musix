# 05 Triads and Drop Chords

This module covers two related guitar ideas:

- **Triads** as compact chord units across small string groups
- **Drop chords**, especially **Drop 2** and **Drop 3**, as a voicing logic used to spread chords across the fretboard

This module is guitar-specific and assumes the fretboard coordinates and standard tuning from `01-fretboard-layout-and-standard-tuning`.

## 1. Triads on Guitar

A **triad** is a three-note chord: root, third, and fifth. On guitar, triads are especially useful because:

- They are small enough to fit cleanly across fewer strings.
- They reveal the harmonic skeleton of larger chords.
- They can be moved, inverted, and voiced in many ways across the fretboard.
- They connect directly to arpeggios, comping, and melodic harmony.

A full six-string chord often contains extra notes or doublings beyond the triad; the triad is the minimal harmonic core.

## 2. Triad Types and Formulas

Triads are built from the same formulas used in the instrument-agnostic chord construction module:

- **Major:** $1 - 3 - 5$
- **Minor:** $1 - \flat 3 - 5$
- **Diminished:** $1 - \flat 3 - \flat 5$
- **Augmented:** $1 - 3 - \sharp 5$

On guitar, what changes is not the formula but the *voicing*: which strings carry which notes, in which octave, and in what order.

## 3. Triad String Groups

Because triads occupy three notes, guitarists often think in **string groups** of three adjacent strings:

- 6–5–4 (low group)
- 5–4–3
- 4–3–2
- 3–2–1 (high group)

Each group offers different voicing characteristics. Lower groups tend to sound fuller and darker; higher groups tend to sound brighter and more delicate.

A triad rooted on a given pitch can often be played in multiple string groups. That is one reason the guitar has so many ways to voice the same chord.

## 4. Root Position, First Inversion, Second Inversion

Triads can be inverted, and inversions change the bass note:

- **Root position:** root is lowest
- **First inversion:** third is lowest
- **Second inversion:** fifth is lowest

On guitar, inversions are not just theoretical: they determine which note is in the bass and which shape is playable.

For a C major triad, for example:

- Root position: C–E–G, bass = C
- First inversion: E–G–C, bass = E
- Second inversion: G–C–E, bass = G

The same three notes in a different vertical order become a different voicing with a different bass function.

## 5. Triad Voicing Logic

When voicing a triad on guitar:

- Keep the notes in correct chord order relative to the bass when inversion matters.
- Avoid muddy low-register spacing when not needed.
- Use open strings or doublings when they suit the musical context.
- Choose the string group that fits the register and the surrounding harmony.

Triads are often voiced so that the most important harmonic notes — usually the third (quality) and the root (identity) — are clearly present.

## 6. ASCII Triad Sketch

A schematic triad across three strings can be drawn as:

```
String 3: ●  (third)
String 2: ●  (fifth)
String 1: ●  (root)
```

Or across a lower group:

```
String 6: ●  (root)
String 5: ●  (third)
String 4: ●  (fifth)
```

These are abstract sketches showing note order and group, not literal fret numbers. Real locations depend on key, position, and inversion.

## 7. Drop Chords: The General Idea

**Drop chords** are a voicing method whose name comes from voice-leading: take a close-voiced chord in a given inversion, then “drop” one of the upper voices down an octave.

In simple terms:

- Start with a compact stack of chord tones.
- Move one voice down an octave to create a wider spread voicing.
- The result is a voicing that occupies more space in the register, often more playable and sonically interesting on guitar.

On guitar, drop voicings are especially useful because the fretboard is wide and spread across six strings.

## 8. Drop 2 Voicings

A **Drop 2** voicing takes a close chord and drops the **second note from the top** down an octave.

- The “second from the top” means the voice just below the highest note in the close stack.
- The resulting voicing is wider and often sits nicely across several strings.
- Drop 2 voicings are a core resource in jazz, arranging, and comping.

A close-voiced chord is first defined in terms of its voice order; Drop 2 then expands it.

### Why It Matters on Guitar

- Drop 2 gives large, usable voicings without stacking every note in a tight cluster.
- It can place chord tones on different strings in a balanced way.
- It creates different bass and inner-voice relationships, affecting both function and color.

### Example Idea: C Major

If a close C major voicing is:

$$ C - E - G - B $$

arranged from lowest to highest, Drop 2 might drop the second-from-top voice (G) down an octave, producing a wider voicing whose notes lie in a different register than the original close stack.

The exact string and fret realization depends on register and the target voicing range; the concept is the octave drop, not a single fixed shape.

## 9. Drop 3 Voicings

A **Drop 3** voicing takes a close chord and drops the **third voice from the top** down an octave.

- The selected voice is lower in the stack than the Drop 2 voice.
- The resulting voicing tends to spread the inner structure more than Drop 2 in some contexts.
- Drop 3 voicings can be especially useful for creating a strong bass area and a more open texture.

Again, the conceptual operation is the same: take a close layout, pick a voice rank from the top, and drop that voice an octave.

## 10. Comparing Drop 2 and Drop 3

| Feature           | Drop 2                          | Drop 3                         |
|-------------------|---------------------------------|--------------------------------|
| Voice dropped     | Second from top                | Third from top                 |
| Typical spread    | Wide, practical across strings | More internal expansion       |
| Common use cases  | Jazz comping, arranging        | Voicings needing broader texture |
| Bass effect       | Often strong, controlled       | Often even lower/broader      |

Both are tools for turning a compact chord idea into an expansive fretboard voicing while preserving the chord’s identity.

## 11. Triad-Based Drop Voicings on Guitar

Because triads are small, they are a natural entry point into drop logic:

- A triad can be voiced in root position, first inversion, or second inversion.
- Adding a seventh or doubling a note expands the range of possible drop voicings.
- Drop voicings derived from triads can then be moved across the neck using the same coordinate logic and string-group thinking.

In practice, this means the fretboard is populated not only with “open” and “barre” chords, but also with many custom voicings derived from triad + drop logic.

## 12. A Practical Fretboard Approach

Work through a chord like this:

1. Name the chord quality and extend if needed (triad, 7th, etc.).
2. Choose a string group and register.
3. Decide on inversion and bass note according to the musical context.
4. Convert a close idea into a Drop 2 or Drop 3 voicing if you want more spread.
5. Learn the voicing’s notes by name, not just the shape.
6. Practice moving the voicing up and down through positions, keeping the notes and function clear.

This keeps drop voicings tied to harmony instead of becoming isolated shapes.

## 13. Relationship to CAGED and Scale Systems

Drop voicings interact with CAGED (see `02-caged-system-and-movable-shapes`) and scale systems (see `03-scale-box-patterns-and-3nps`) because:

- The same fretboard regions contain triad fragments and drop voicings.
- Roots and inversions determine where a voicing can sit.
- Voice-leading between drop voicings often follows the same principles as smooth voice leading in harmony (see `03-voice-leading`).

CAGED shapes, triads, and drop voicings all live in the same coordinate space; they are different ways to slice it.

## 14. Summary

- Triads are the compact three-note core of guitar harmony, voiced across small string groups.
- Inversions change the bass note and the playable shape.
- Drop chords expand close voicings by dropping a chosen voice an octave; Drop 2 drops the second-from-top voice, Drop 3 the third-from-top.
- Drop voicings create practical, wide, balanced chords across the fretboard.
- Triad-based drop logic connects directly to CAGED regions, scale patterns, and voice-leading principles.
