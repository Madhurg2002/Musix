# 03 Voice Leading

**Voice leading** is the art and technique of moving individual lines — voices — from one chord to the next in a smooth, logical, and musically satisfying way. Good voice leading minimizes awkward leaps, avoids unintended counterpoint errors, and clarifies harmonic function.

This module is instrument-agnostic.

## 1. What Voice Leading Means

A chord is not just a set of pitches; it is a collection of **voices** that can move independently. Voice leading describes how each voice moves from one harmony to the next. Even when a chord is played as a block, there is an implicit voicing and implied voice leading.

Good voice leading:

- Keeps common tones where possible
- Moves other voices by the smallest sensible intervals
- Respects the intended harmonic function
- Avoids awkward melodic motion in individual lines

## 2. Voices and Voicing

In four-part writing (a classic teaching framework), the voices are often labeled:

- **Soprano** (highest)
- **Alto**
- **Tenor**
- **Bass** (lowest)

In broader terms, any arrangement of notes from a chord into separate lines — whether in an ensemble, arrangement, or composition — involves voice leading decisions.

**Voicing** is the choice of which octave each chord tone occupies and which notes are doubled, omitted, or spread. Voicing choices directly affect what voice leading is possible and smooth.

## 3. The Basic Principles

### Smooth Motion

Prefer stepwise motion or common-tone retention when moving between chords. Large leaps in individual voices draw attention and may break the sense of flow.

### Common Tones

If a pitch is shared between two chords, keeping that pitch in the same voice anchors the progression. For example, I and vi share several notes, so voice leading between them is naturally smooth.

### Contrary and Similar Motion

- **Contrary motion:** voices move in opposite directions — generally safe and often elegant.
- **Similar motion:** voices move in the same direction — can be useful but needs care to avoid parallels.
- **Parallel motion:** voices move in the same direction by the same interval — powerful but must be controlled to avoid prohibited parallels (especially parallel fifths and octaves in traditional styles).

### Bass Motion

The bass often moves with the harmonic root or functional purpose in mind (e.g., V → I bass motion is central to authentic cadence). In many styles, bass motion is an especially clear signpost of harmonic direction.

## 4. Leading Tones and Resolution

One of the most important voice-leading events is the resolution of the **leading tone** (scale degree 7) to the tonic (1), especially in V → I.

In a V7 → I cadence:

- The leading tone typically resolves upward to the tonic.
- The chordal seventh (in V7) resolves downward by step.
- The third of V often goes to the root or third of I depending on context and voicing.

These tendencies explain why V → I feels so conclusive — voice leading gives the resolution its pull.

## 5. Avoidance of Forbidden Parallels

In traditional counterpoint and many classical styles:

- **Parallel perfect fifths and octaves** between adjacent voices are avoided, especially in strict styles, because they weaken the independence of voices.
- This does not mean all parallel motion is bad; parallel thirds, sixths, and other intervals are common and idiomatic.

In folk, rock, pop, and jazz contexts, parallels and block-chord motion are perfectly acceptable, but voice-leading awareness still improves coherence.

## 6. Voice Leading in Different Styles

### Classical / Counterpoint

- Emphasizes independence of voices.
- Controls parallels, spacing, and ranges.
- Uses species counterpoint as a pedagogical framework.

### Common-Practice Tonality

- Follows functional progressions.
- Resolves leading tones and sevenths appropriately.
- Balances smoothness with clear harmonic direction.

### Jazz and Popular Music

- Often uses block chords, close voicings, and doubling.
- Parallel movement and guide tones are common.
- Voice leading is looser but still shapes melodic and harmonic flow.

### Arranging and Orchestration

- Voice leading extends to instrumental layout, doubling, spacing, and ranges.
- Each instrument's register affects how smooth and balanced the progression feels.

## 7. Guide Tones

**Guide tones** are chord tones that define the harmonic identity most clearly:

- For triads: typically the third and the seventh (when present).
- For seventh chords: especially the third and the seventh, because they define major/minor quality and dominant function.

Connecting guide tones smoothly between chords is a powerful technique, especially in jazz and in arranging.

For example, in a ii–V–I:

- The third of ii (the minor third) often moves to the seventh of V.
- The seventh of V resolves to the third of I.

This creates a concise, logical line even when other voices change more dramatically.

## 8. Voice Leading in Cadences

Cadences are where voice leading is most explicit:

- **Authentic (V → I):** leading tone rises, seventh falls, bass may move down a fifth or up a fourth.
- **Plagal (IV → I):** often smoother, more static; voice leading is less driven by leading-tone motion.
- **Half cadence (ends on V):** prepares a sense of suspension and continuation.
- **Deceptive (V → vi):** the expected tonic root is avoided; voice leading reroutes to the vi chord, often with a rising bass or altered upper voices.

Voice-leading expectations are why cadences "feel" the way they do.

## 9. Doubling and Omission of Chord Tones

When voicing chords:

- **Root** is often doubled in many textures, especially with bass foundation.
- **Third** defines quality and is usually included if the chord's character matters.
- **Fifth** can be omitted or doubled depending on spacing and function.
- **Seventh** must be handled carefully because its resolution affects voice leading.

Omitting a tone is often better than moving it awkwardly or creating low interval problems.

## 10. Spacing, Register, and Textures

Voice leading is affected by where each voice sits:

- Tightly clustered **close voicings** sound compact and focused.
- **Open voicings** spread the chord over a wider range, often creating a more resonant, less muddy texture.

When voices are too close in the lower register, the texture may become muddy; in the upper register, too much space may weaken cohesion. Good voice leading balances these considerations.

## 11. Inversions and Bass Motion

Chord inversions change the bass note and thus the implied bass motion, even when upper voices are arranged similarly.

- First inversion brings the third to the bass; the bass typically moves more smoothly stepwise.
- Second inversion puts the fifth in the bass, often producing a less stable sound that tends toward resolution.
- Third inversion (in seventh chords) places the seventh in the bass, driving strong resolution.

Inversions are a voice-leading tool: they let harmonic function coexist with smooth bass and inner-voice motion.

## 12. Figured Bass and Voice Leading Tradition

**Figured bass** notation was developed to indicate how to fill in harmony above a given bass note. It is a historical voice-leading system that encodes:

- Which intervals above the bass are expected
- How to handle inversions
- How to realize a harmonic framework in a flexible way

Even though modern analysis uses Roman numerals, figured bass remains an important historical and pedagogical link between bass motion and upper-voice realization.

## 13. Connection Between Voice Leading and Analysis

Voice leading helps explain *why* certain progressions work and why others feel awkward. In analysis, noticing:

- common tones
- stepwise motion
- leading-tone and seventh resolution
- parallelisms and their effect

adds depth beyond simply labeling chords.

## 14. Summary

- Voice leading is the movement of individual lines between harmonies.
- Smoothness, common tones, stepwise motion, and controlled parallels are the core principles.
- Leading-tone and seventh resolution are especially important in tonal cadences.
- Guide tones and bass motion give a concise framework for connecting chords.
- Voice-leading expectations differ by style, but the goal of coherent motion is constant.
- Voice leading links harmonic function to the musical surface and deepens analysis.
