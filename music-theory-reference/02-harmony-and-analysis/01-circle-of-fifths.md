# 01 Circle of Fifths

The **circle of fifths** is a visual and conceptual tool that organizes key relationships by perfect fifths. It is central to understanding key signatures, modulation, chord progressions, and harmonic relationships across tonal music.

This module is instrument-agnostic.

## 1. What the Circle Represents

The circle arranges the 12 pitch classes in a circular layout, each step moving by a **perfect fifth** (7 semitones) clockwise. Moving counterclockwise corresponds to **perfect fourths** (or fifths in the opposite direction).

The layout typically shows:

- 12 keys grouped in sharps and flats
- Relative key relationships
- Number of sharps or flats in each key signature
- The pairing of relative major/minor keys

## 2. Moving by Fifths

Starting from C and moving clockwise by perfect fifths:

$$ C \rightarrow G \rightarrow D \rightarrow A \rightarrow E \rightarrow B \rightarrow F♯ \rightarrow C♯ \rightarrow G♯ \rightarrow D♯ \rightarrow A♯ \rightarrow F $$

Each step adds one sharp (eventually) to the key signature (or conceptually removes a flat).

Starting from C and moving counterclockwise:

$$ C \rightarrow F \rightarrow B♭ \rightarrow E♭ \rightarrow A♭ \rightarrow D♭ \rightarrow G♭ \rightarrow C♭ \rightarrow F♭ \rightarrow B♭♭ \rightarrow E♭♭ \rightarrow A♭♭ $$

Each step adds one flat to the key signature.

## 3. Key Signatures and the Circle

The circle maps directly onto standard key signatures:

- C major / A minor: no sharps/flats
- G major / E minor: 1 sharp
- D major / B minor: 2 sharps
- A major / F♯ minor: 3 sharps
- E major / C♯ minor: 4 sharps
- B major / G♯ minor: 5 sharps
- F♯ major / D♯ minor: 6 sharps
- C♯ major / A♯ minor: 7 sharps

On the flat side:

- F major / D minor: 1 flat
- B♭ major / G minor: 2 flats
- E♭ major / C minor: 3 flats
- A♭ major / F minor: 4 flats
- D♭ major / B♭ minor: 5 flats
- G♭ major / E♭ minor: 6 flats
- C♭ major / A♭ minor: 7 flats

The circle helps remember these sequences and the relationships among keys.

## 4. Relative Major and Minor

Each major key has a **relative minor** a minor third below (or equivalently a minor sixth above, same pitch classes). These share the same key signature.

Examples:

- C major ↔ A minor
- G major ↔ E minor
- F major ↔ D minor
- B♭ major ↔ G minor

On the circle, these are typically drawn at the same position or adjacent, depending on the diagram.

## 5. Circle Geometry and Neighbors

Adjacent keys on the circle share many pitch classes and are closely related:

- Closely related keys differ by at most one accidental.
- Moving to a neighbor is a common, smooth modulation.

This makes the circle useful for:

- Planning modulations
- Understanding chord relationships
- Choosing secondary dominants or borrowed chords
- Visualizing the tonal "distance" between keys

## 6. Tonal Relationships on the Circle

Beyond simple key signatures, the circle shows:

- **Tonic–Dominant relationships:** A key's dominant is a fifth clockwise.
- **Subdominant relationships:** A key's subdominant is a fifth counterclockwise.
- **Relative minor/major:** Shares key signature and many chords.

These relationships are why many progressions emphasize dominant-to-tonic motion (V → I), and why a circle-of-fifths layout is so effective for understanding tonal structure.

## 7. Chord Relationships and the Circle

The circle is also used at the chord level:

- The circle-of-fifths progression (e.g., descending by fifths: vi → ii → V → I) creates a powerful harmonic pull.
- Common progressions often move through closely related keys and chords that lie near each other on the circle.

In diatonic harmony, the primary triads of a major key are often conceptualized as:

- I (tonic)
- IV (subdominant)
- V (dominant)

and the circle helps explain why IV and V are especially closely tied to I.

## 8. Modulating with the Circle

Because adjacent keys share many pitch classes and are separated by a single accidental, they are the most common targets for modulation. Common paths:

- Move to the dominant key (clockwise).
- Move to the subdominant key (counterclockwise).
- Move to the relative minor/major (shared key signature).

More distant modulations (by several steps on the circle) are more dramatic and perceptible.

## 9. Circle and Serial / At times Non-Tonal Contexts

Although the circle is fundamentally a tool for tonal thinking, it still informs:

- Pitch-class set visualization (mapping intervals around the clock face).
- Symmetrical divisions (for example, the circle helps visualize whole-tone and octatonic subsets).
- Understanding why certain intervals (especially fifths and fourths) are so structurally important.

However, in atonal or highly chromatic contexts, the circle is a simplification, not a full description.

## 10. Common Mnemonics

A standard mnemonic for the sharp side is:

$$ C\; G\; D\; A\; E\; B\; F♯ $$

Often remembered as a phrase or sequence of letters, with each step adding a sharp.

For the flat side, the order of flats is often given as:

$$ B\; E\; A\; D\; G\; C\; F $$

which is the reverse of the order of sharps, reflecting the symmetry of the circle.

## 11. Summary

- The circle of fifths arranges the 12 pitch classes by perfect fifths.
- It organizes key signatures, relative major/minor pairs, and closely related keys.
- Clockwise adds sharps; counterclockwise adds flats.
- It visualizes harmonic relationships useful for modulation, progressions, and chord analysis.
- Even outside tonal contexts, the circle remains a useful way to think about fifth/fourth symmetry and pitch-class relationships.
